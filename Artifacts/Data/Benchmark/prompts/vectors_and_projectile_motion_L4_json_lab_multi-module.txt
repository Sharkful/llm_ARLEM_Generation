## Field
physics

## Course context
General Physics I (Mechanics), 100-level algebra-based undergraduate course

## Topic description
An exploration of vectors as the mathematical tool for representing
quantities with both magnitude and direction, and the application of
vector operations to two-dimensional projectile motion. Students work
first with vectors as abstract objects: they see how two vectors add
tip-to-tail to produce a resultant, how a single vector can be
decomposed into components along chosen axes (typically the horizontal
x-axis and vertical y-axis), and how the magnitude and direction of a
vector relate to its components through right-triangle trigonometry.
The lab then applies these operations to projectile motion. A
projectile launched at an arbitrary angle has an initial velocity
vector that can be decomposed into a horizontal component (which
remains constant throughout the flight, since no horizontal force
acts on the projectile) and a vertical component (which changes
steadily under the influence of gravity, decreasing during ascent
and increasing in the downward direction during descent). Students
see that the horizontal and vertical motions are independent of one
another — a counterintuitive but central insight of classical
mechanics — and that the parabolic trajectory of the projectile
emerges from the combination of constant horizontal velocity and
uniformly accelerated vertical motion. Students manipulate the
initial launch angle and speed and observe how these choices change
the trajectory, range, and maximum height of the projectile.

## Learning objectives
- Students will represent a two-dimensional vector by its magnitude and direction, and equivalently by its horizontal and vertical components.
- Students will add two or more vectors graphically using the tip-to-tail method and identify the resultant vector.
- Students will decompose a vector into components along the x- and y-axes using right-triangle trigonometry, and reconstruct the original vector from its components.
- Students will explain why the horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent of one another, given that gravity acts only in the vertical direction.
- Students will describe how the horizontal velocity, vertical velocity, and position of a projectile change over the course of its flight, and identify the moment of maximum height as the point where the vertical velocity is zero.
- Students will predict how changes in initial launch angle and launch speed affect the trajectory, range, and maximum height of a projectile, and identify the launch angle that produces maximum range for a given speed.

## Detailed script
The lab opens with an introduction to vectors. A single arrow appears
floating in the 3D workspace, with its tail at a fixed origin point and
its head extending outward in some direction. The arrow is rendered with
a clearly visible tail, shaft, and head. Two numerical labels float near
the arrow: a magnitude value (the length of the arrow) and a direction
indicator (a small set of angle measurements relative to the x, y, and
z reference axes that are also visible in the workspace as colored
ground lines). Students can grab the arrow's head and drag it around in
3D space; as they do, the magnitude and direction values update in real
time. Narration introduces vectors as quantities with both a size (the
magnitude) and a direction in space, distinct from scalar quantities
like temperature or mass that have only a size. Students rotate around
the vector to view it from any angle and confirm that the underlying
arrow does not change — only their viewpoint on it.

The second scene introduces vector addition by the tip-to-tail method.
Two vectors appear in the workspace, drawn in different colors (for
example, blue for the first vector and green for the second). The
first vector starts at the origin; the second vector starts at the
head of the first, placed tip-to-tail. A third arrow, the resultant,
is drawn from the tail of the first to the head of the second in a
third color (for example, red). Students can grab and reorient either
the blue or the green vector; the resultant updates continuously to
remain anchored from the origin to the head of the second vector.
Narration explains that vector addition is the operation that produces
the resultant, and that the order in which the two vectors are added
does not matter — the resultant is the same whether the green is laid
on top of the blue or vice versa. Students confirm this by swapping
the order of the two vectors and observing that the resultant points
to the same place.

The third scene introduces vector decomposition by treating it as
reversed addition. A single vector appears in the workspace, drawn in
a neutral color. Three smaller component arrows then emerge from the
origin, one along each axis (x, y, z), color-coded to match the axes.
The component arrows lie tip-to-tail in sequence: the x component
starts at the origin, the y component starts at the tip of x, and the
z component starts at the tip of y, terminating at the head of the
original vector. Narration introduces this as decomposition — breaking
the original vector down into pieces along the chosen axes — and
emphasizes that the three components add up tip-to-tail to reproduce
the original. Students can grab and resize each component arrow
individually, lengthening or shortening it along its axis; as they do,
the original vector updates to reflect the new sum. The narration ties
this directly back to the previous scene: decomposition is just
addition viewed in reverse — instead of adding two vectors to get a
resultant, the resultant has been broken into three vectors that add
back to it. A brief mention notes that the magnitudes of the
components relate to the original vector's magnitude through the
three-dimensional Pythagorean theorem, and in two dimensions the
components and magnitude form a right triangle.

The fourth scene transitions from abstract vectors to projectile
motion. The 3D vector workspace gives way to a 2D vertical plane,
rendered as a clearly visible surface in the AR environment with
horizontal and vertical axes labeled. Narration explains that the
vector tools just introduced will now be applied to a specific
physical problem: the motion of a projectile launched into the air.
Because the projectile experiences gravity (which acts only
downward) and no horizontal force, the entire motion takes place in
a vertical plane that contains the initial velocity vector — so
although the world is three-dimensional, the projectile problem is
genuinely two-dimensional. Students see a small projectile (a ball)
resting on the ground line at the left side of the plane, with a
velocity vector arrow drawn from the ball pointing up and to the
right at some initial angle.

The fifth scene is the projectile motion demonstration. The initial
velocity vector is shown decomposed into two component arrows: a
horizontal arrow (pointing right) and a vertical arrow (pointing
up), color-coded to distinguish them from the original velocity.
Narration explains that the horizontal component represents the
ball's rightward velocity at launch, and the vertical component
represents the ball's upward velocity at launch. The ball is
launched. As it flies, both component arrows are drawn on the ball
in real time and update continuously: the horizontal arrow stays the
same length throughout the flight, while the vertical arrow shrinks
during the ascent, becomes zero at the peak of the trajectory, then
grows downward during the descent. The ball traces a parabolic path,
which is drawn behind it as a faint trail. Students watch the full
flight from a default side view; controls allow them to switch to an
overhead view (looking straight down the y-axis, where the parabola
disappears and the motion appears as a straight horizontal line) and
to other angles in between. Narration emphasizes the central
insight: the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The
horizontal motion is unaffected by gravity — the ball moves
rightward at a constant velocity, exactly as it would if there were
no gravity at all. The vertical motion is exactly what a ball
thrown straight up would do — it slows, stops, and falls. The
parabolic trajectory is just these two motions happening at the
same time.

The sixth scene is the discovery of the maximum-range result.
Students are given controls to set the initial launch angle (from
0 to 90 degrees above horizontal) and the launch speed. The
projectile launches each time the student changes the parameters
and presses launch; the trajectory is drawn and the range (the
horizontal distance from launch to landing on the same elevation)
is displayed. Students try various combinations and observe how
range changes. A small history panel records the angle, speed, and
range of each launch, so students can compare across attempts. As
they explore, they discover that for any fixed launch speed, the
range is maximized when the launch angle is 45 degrees, and that
angles symmetric about 45 degrees (for example, 30 and 60, or 20
and 70) produce the same range. Once students have made a few
launches in the 35-55 degree range, narration confirms the result
and explains it: the range is maximized when the horizontal and
vertical components of the initial velocity are equal, which occurs
at 45 degrees. If a student happens to launch at or very close to
45 degrees on one of their first few attempts, narration confirms
the result early rather than waiting — there is no need to keep
the student exploring if they have already found the maximum.

The seventh scene is a targeting challenge. A target appears at a
fixed location on the ground plane, some distance away from the
launch point and at the same elevation. Students must set the
launch angle and speed to hit the target. The challenge repeats
several times with the target placed at different distances; each
successful hit advances to the next target. Narration notes that
for any given target distance there are typically two angle
solutions — a complementary pair such as 30 degrees and 60 degrees
— and invites students to find both for at least one target if
they want to, though only a single hit is required to advance.
The targeting challenge ties the vector decomposition skills from
the abstract half directly to the practical task of predicting
projectile behavior.

The eighth scene is a free-exploration sandbox. The constraints of
the structured scenes are removed, and students gain control over
every parameter of the projectile motion problem within reasonable
bounds: initial launch angle, launch speed, initial height (the
ball can now be launched from a platform at any height from ground
level up to approximately 50 meters), and gravity strength (which
can be scaled from zero up to five times Earth's gravity). The
targeting elements are gone; students simply launch projectiles
and watch the trajectories, with the live component arrows still
drawn. Narration suggests a few exploratory questions for students
to investigate: what happens to the range when gravity is halved?
what does the trajectory look like under very strong gravity? what
if the projectile is launched from a 50-meter platform — does 45
degrees still maximize range, or does the optimal angle shift?
This scene gives students a chance to consolidate intuition by
playing with the system.

The lab closes with a recap. A summary screen lists the six concept
areas covered — vectors as quantities with magnitude and direction,
vector addition by the tip-to-tail method, vector decomposition as
reversed addition along chosen axes, the application of vector
decomposition to projectile motion, the independence of horizontal
and vertical motion under gravity, and the 45-degree maximum range
result — with each area highlighted in turn as the narration
reviews it.
