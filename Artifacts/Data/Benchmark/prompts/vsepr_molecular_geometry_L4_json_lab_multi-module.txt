## Field
chemistry

## Course context
General Chemistry I, 100-level undergraduate course (majors and non-majors)

## Topic description
An exploration of VSEPR theory and how it predicts the three-dimensional
geometry of covalent molecules. Students start from a Lewis structure and
apply the central idea of VSEPR — that electron pairs around a central
atom arrange themselves to minimize mutual repulsion — to determine the
arrangement of bonding and lone pairs in space. The lab walks through
the five fundamental electron-domain geometries (linear, trigonal planar,
tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral) and shows how lone pairs
modify the molecular geometry within each, producing the full set of
common shapes: bent, trigonal pyramidal, seesaw, T-shaped, square planar,
and square pyramidal. Students manipulate electron domains around a
central atom and watch the geometry settle into its minimum-repulsion
configuration, then compare to real molecules — water, ammonia, methane,
PF5, SF6, XeF4 — to see VSEPR predictions matched against observed
structures. The lab also addresses why lone pairs occupy more angular
space than bonding pairs, producing the slight bond-angle deviations
from the idealized geometries (the 104.5° angle in water versus 109.5°
in methane).

## Learning objectives
- Students will determine the steric number of a central atom from its Lewis structure by counting bonding pairs and lone pairs.
- Students will predict the electron-domain geometry of a molecule from its steric number, identifying the linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, or octahedral arrangement.
- Students will distinguish electron-domain geometry from molecular geometry, and identify the molecular shape produced when one or more domains is a lone pair.
- Students will explain why lone pairs occupy more angular space than bonding pairs, and predict the direction of bond-angle deviations from idealized values.
- Students will apply VSEPR predictions to specific molecules and compare predicted geometries to experimentally observed structures.

## Detailed script
The lab opens with a setup scene introducing VSEPR theory. A central atom
appears at the center of the workspace, rendered as a labeled sphere. The
scene introduces the core idea through narration: electrons in bonding
pairs and lone pairs around a central atom repel one another, and the
molecule adopts whatever geometry places these electron domains as far
apart as possible. A small reference panel off to the side shows a sample
Lewis structure (water, drawn flat) and reminds students how to count
electron domains from it: each single bond, double bond, triple bond, or
lone pair counts as one domain. Narration notes that VSEPR takes a flat
Lewis structure as input and predicts the actual three-dimensional shape.

The next scene demonstrates the minimum-repulsion principle directly. Two
electron domains — rendered as mostly-opaque lobed clouds extending out
from the central atom — appear at arbitrary starting positions. Students
watch the domains drift apart, repelled by one another, until they settle
180 degrees apart in a linear arrangement. A third domain is added; the
three rearrange themselves into a trigonal planar configuration at 120
degrees. A fourth produces a tetrahedron at 109.5 degrees. With each
addition, faint repulsion vectors appear briefly between domains during
the rearrangement and fade once the geometry stabilizes. Narration ties
each settled configuration to its electron-domain geometry name.

The third scene completes the geometry catalog. Building from the
tetrahedron, a fifth electron domain is added; the geometry rearranges
into trigonal bipyramidal, with three equatorial domains at 120 degrees
and two axial domains at 90 degrees to the equatorial plane. A sixth
domain produces an octahedral arrangement with all neighbors at 90
degrees. Students can rotate the molecule freely and see the geometry
from any angle. A summary panel at the end of the scene shows all five
electron-domain geometries side by side with their domain counts (2, 3,
4, 5, 6) and characteristic angles (180°, 120°, 109.5°, 90°/120°, 90°).

The fourth scene introduces the distinction between electron-domain
geometry and molecular geometry. The tetrahedral arrangement returns,
this time with three bonding domains shown as solid bonds extending to
terminal atoms (representing methane: CH4), while a label confirms the
electron-domain geometry as tetrahedral and the molecular geometry as
tetrahedral — they match when all domains are bonding. Then one of the
bonded atoms is replaced with a lone pair: a hydrogen disappears and the
domain in its place becomes a translucent lone-pair cloud (representing
ammonia: NH3). The label updates: electron-domain geometry is still
tetrahedral, but molecular geometry — the shape traced by the atoms
alone — is now trigonal pyramidal. A second hydrogen is replaced with a
lone pair (water: H2O); the molecular geometry becomes bent. Narration
emphasizes that the underlying electron-domain geometry has stayed
tetrahedral throughout, but the visible shape of the molecule has
changed because lone pairs are not "seen" as part of the molecular
shape.

The fifth scene examines lone pair angular asymmetry. The three
molecules from the previous scene — methane, ammonia, water — are
displayed side by side, with bond angles labeled (109.5°, 107°,
104.5°). The lone-pair clouds in ammonia and water are rendered larger
than the bonding domains, visually conveying that they occupy more
angular space. Narration explains that lone pairs are held only by the
central atom rather than shared between two nuclei, so they spread out
more and compress the remaining bonding angles. Students can toggle a
view that overlays all three molecules at the central atom to directly
compare how the H-X-H angle decreases as more lone pairs are added.

The sixth scene is a gallery of real molecules covering all five
electron-domain geometries. Six molecules appear arranged in space:
CO2 (linear), BF3 (trigonal planar), CH4 (tetrahedral), PF5 (trigonal
bipyramidal), SF6 (octahedral), and three lone-pair-containing examples
worth highlighting separately — NH3 (trigonal pyramidal), H2O (bent),
and XeF4 (square planar). Students can approach each molecule, see its
Lewis structure displayed alongside its 3D structure, and confirm the
count of bonding domains and lone pairs around the central atom.
Narration walks through each example, naming the electron-domain
geometry, the molecular geometry, and the bond angles.

The seventh scene is the first prediction activity: drag the domains.
A central atom appears with a specified number of electron domains
(starting with four) floating loosely around it, not yet in any
configuration. Students must drag the domains into the correct
geometry. The system checks the angular arrangement; when domains are
positioned within tolerance of the correct angles, the configuration
snaps into place and is confirmed. The challenge repeats with two,
three, five, and six domains. After the all-bonding versions, the
challenge progresses to mixed configurations — for example, four
domains where one is a lone pair, requiring students to recognize
that the lone pair occupies a tetrahedral position even though the
resulting molecular geometry is trigonal pyramidal.

The eighth scene is the second prediction activity: pick from
options. A Lewis structure of an unfamiliar molecule appears (for
example, SF4, ClF3, BrF5, or I3-). Three or four candidate 3D
structures are displayed alongside. Students examine the Lewis
structure, determine the steric number and lone pair count, and
select the matching 3D geometry. After each selection, the correct
answer is confirmed or corrected, and narration walks through the
reasoning: count the domains, identify the electron-domain geometry,
account for lone pairs, name the molecular geometry. The challenge
repeats for several molecules, sampling all five electron-domain
geometries.

The lab closes with a recap. A summary screen lists the five concept
areas covered — the minimum-repulsion principle, the five electron-
domain geometries and their angles, the distinction between
electron-domain and molecular geometry, lone pair angular asymmetry,
and the application to real molecules — with each area highlighted in
turn as the narration reviews it.
