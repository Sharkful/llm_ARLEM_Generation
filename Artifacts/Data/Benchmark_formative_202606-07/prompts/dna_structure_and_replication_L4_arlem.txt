## Field
biology

## Course context
Introductory Molecular Biology, 200-level undergraduate course (biology and biochemistry majors)

## Topic description
An exploration of the three-dimensional structure of DNA and the
semiconservative process by which it replicates. Students examine the
double helix and identify its components: two antiparallel sugar-
phosphate backbones spiraling around a common axis, with the four
nitrogenous bases (adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine) projecting
inward and pairing across the helix according to the complementary
base-pairing rules — adenine with thymine via two hydrogen bonds,
guanine with cytosine via three. Students see how the antiparallel
orientation arises from the directionality of the sugar-phosphate
backbone, with one strand running 5' to 3' and the complementary
strand running 3' to 5'. The lab then walks through DNA replication:
helicase unwinds the double helix at the replication fork, single-
strand binding proteins stabilize the exposed strands, and DNA
polymerase synthesizes new strands using each parental strand as a
template. Because DNA polymerase can only extend a strand in the 5'
to 3' direction, the two new strands are synthesized differently —
the leading strand continuously following the replication fork, the
lagging strand discontinuously in Okazaki fragments that are later
joined by DNA ligase. Students see how the antiparallel structure of
the double helix forces this asymmetry, and why the process is called
semiconservative: each daughter molecule contains one original
parental strand and one newly-synthesized strand.

## Learning objectives
- Students will identify the structural components of DNA — the sugar-phosphate backbone, the four nitrogenous bases, and the hydrogen bonds between base pairs — and describe how they assemble into the double helix.
- Students will apply the complementary base-pairing rules (A with T, G with C) to determine the sequence of one strand given the sequence of the other, and explain why these specific pairings occur (two hydrogen bonds for A-T, three for G-C).
- Students will describe the antiparallel orientation of the two strands of DNA and explain how the 5'-to-3' directionality of each sugar-phosphate backbone determines this arrangement.
- Students will trace the action of helicase, single-strand binding proteins, DNA polymerase, and DNA ligase at the replication fork, identifying which enzyme performs which function.
- Students will explain why DNA replication produces a leading strand synthesized continuously and a lagging strand synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, relating this asymmetry to the unidirectional 5'-to-3' activity of DNA polymerase on antiparallel templates.
- Students will articulate why DNA replication is described as semiconservative, and predict the composition of daughter molecules after one and two rounds of replication.

## Detailed script
The lab opens with a schematic ladder representation of DNA. A flat,
ladder-like structure appears in the workspace: two vertical lines
represent the sugar-phosphate backbones, and horizontal rungs between
them represent base pairs. Each rung is composed of two colored
segments meeting in the middle, color-coded by base identity (for
example, adenine red, thymine blue, guanine yellow, cytosine green).
The bases are labeled with their one-letter abbreviations. Narration
introduces DNA as a polymer with a regular structure: a repeating
sugar-phosphate backbone with nitrogenous bases projecting inward to
pair with their complements on the opposite strand. The schematic
makes the base-pairing pattern clear before any geometric complexity
is introduced.

The second scene transitions from schematic to realistic geometry.
The ladder twists into the familiar double-helix shape, with the
backbones spiraling around a common axis and the base pairs stacking
along the interior. A toggle lets students switch between the
schematic ladder and the realistic helix at any time during this
scene, building the mental mapping between the two representations.
Narration notes that the helix completes one full turn approximately
every ten base pairs, and that the helical structure is what real
DNA looks like in cells. The major and minor grooves are visible but
not labeled or discussed — they are simply features of the geometry
that students can observe.

The third scene introduces the antiparallel orientation and 5'-to-3'
directionality. The realistic helix is shown with arrows along each
backbone indicating direction: one strand's arrow points up, the
other's points down. Labels appear at the ends of each strand
identifying the 5' and 3' termini, with brief narration explaining
that these labels refer to the carbon atoms of the sugar ring at the
ends of each backbone. The narration emphasizes that the two strands
always run in opposite directions, and that this antiparallel
arrangement is not optional — it is forced by the directional
chemistry of the sugar-phosphate backbone. Students can rotate the
helix and confirm the arrows from any angle.

The fourth scene is an interactive base-pairing activity. A partial
ladder structure appears: one complete strand on the left with its
bases labeled (for example, a sequence like 5'-ATGCCAGT-3'), and an
empty strand on the right with backbone present but no bases attached.
Loose bases (adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine) are arranged in a
palette below the structure. Students drag bases from the palette and
attach them to the empty strand to complete the complementary sequence.
The system checks each placement: a correct pairing (A with T, G with
C) snaps into place and forms hydrogen bonds, visualized as dashed
lines between the bases — two lines for A-T pairs, three lines for
G-C pairs. An incorrect pairing is rejected with a brief explanation.
After completing the strand, narration confirms the new sequence,
notes its 3'-to-5' direction (opposite to the template), and
emphasizes the connection between hydrogen bond count and pair
strength.

The fifth scene begins the build-up of the replication fork. The
scene returns to a section of the realistic double helix and
introduces helicase as the first enzyme. Helicase appears as a
labeled protein positioned at one end of the helix; it moves along
the DNA and unwinds the two strands as it goes, exposing the bases.
Behind the moving helicase, the strands are separated but no other
proteins are present yet. Narration describes helicase's function:
it breaks the hydrogen bonds between paired bases and opens up the
double helix so the strands can be used as templates. Students watch
helicase work its way along a stretch of DNA.

The sixth scene adds single-strand binding proteins. The scene resumes
from where helicase left off, and as the two strands separate behind
helicase, single-strand binding proteins (SSBs) appear and attach to
each exposed strand. The SSBs are rendered as small labeled spheres
decorating the separated strands. Narration explains that single
strands of DNA are unstable and tend to re-pair or fold on themselves;
the SSBs hold the strands open and accessible so that replication can
proceed. Students can see that without the SSBs, the exposed strands
would collapse back together.

The seventh scene adds DNA polymerase and the leading strand. DNA
polymerase appears on one of the two exposed template strands —
specifically, the one oriented so that synthesis proceeds in the same
direction as the moving replication fork. Narration notes that DNA
polymerase can only build a new strand by adding nucleotides to its
3' end, so polymerase can only travel along a template strand in the
3'-to-5' direction, building the new strand 5' to 3'. On the leading
strand template, the polymerase moves continuously, following the
fork and synthesizing a continuous complementary strand. Free
nucleotides appear from the surrounding solution, pair with the
template, and are added to the growing new strand. A short mention
notes that polymerase needs a primer to begin — a starting point on
which to extend — without dwelling on primase itself. Students watch
the leading strand grow continuously alongside the moving fork.

The eighth scene handles the lagging strand and Okazaki fragments.
On the other exposed template strand — the one whose 3' end points
away from the fork — synthesis cannot proceed continuously, because
polymerase would need to travel away from the fork rather than with
it. Narration explains the problem visually: the polymerase tries to
approach the fork but is forced to work backward away from it,
producing a short fragment. The polymerase then jumps back to the
fork and starts a new fragment, and the cycle repeats. The result is
a series of short pieces (Okazaki fragments) along the lagging strand
template, growing in the opposite direction from the leading strand.
After several fragments have been laid down, DNA ligase appears and
joins them together into a continuous strand. Students see the
fragments seal together at their junctions as ligase moves along.

The ninth scene examines the completed replication fork as a whole.
All five components are now present and active: helicase at the
front, SSBs holding the strands open, leading-strand polymerase
moving continuously, lagging-strand polymerase working in fragments,
and ligase sealing the fragments behind it. Students can rotate the
full fork and watch it operate, identifying each component by name.
A summary panel shows the five enzymes and their functions. Narration
ties the asymmetry between leading and lagging strands back to the
antiparallel structure introduced earlier: because the two template
strands run in opposite directions, and polymerase can only work in
one direction, the two new strands must be synthesized differently.
This is the key conceptual link the lab has been building toward.

The tenth scene is the semiconservative prediction activity. The
scene shows a complete parental DNA molecule with its two strands
rendered in a distinctive color — say, gold — to mark them as the
original material. The molecule replicates once: the strands separate,
new complementary strands are synthesized (rendered in a different
color, say silver), and the result is two daughter molecules, each
containing one gold strand and one silver strand. Narration confirms
that the molecule is "semiconservative" — each daughter conserves
one original parental strand. The two daughter molecules are then
presented as the starting material for round two, and students are
asked to predict the composition of the four molecules that result
from a second round of replication before the lab plays it out.
Students select from options: "all four molecules are half gold, half
silver"; "two molecules are half gold and half silver, two are fully
silver"; or "all four molecules are fully silver." After they select,
the second round plays out, confirming the correct answer (two hybrid
molecules and two fully silver molecules). Narration ties the result
to the Meselson-Stahl experimental design without naming the
experiment.

The lab closes with a recap. A summary screen lists the six concept
areas covered — the double-helix structure with its sugar-phosphate
backbones and bases, the complementary base-pairing rules with
hydrogen-bond counts, the antiparallel 5'-to-3' arrangement of the
two strands, the action of the replication enzymes at the fork, the
leading and lagging strand asymmetry with Okazaki fragments, and the
semiconservative nature of replication — with each area highlighted
in turn as the narration reviews it.
