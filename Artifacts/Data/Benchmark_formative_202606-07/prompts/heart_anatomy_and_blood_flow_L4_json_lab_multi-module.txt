## Field
anatomy

## Course context
Human Anatomy and Physiology I, 200-level undergraduate course (nursing, kinesiology, and pre-health majors)

## Topic description
An exploration of the structural anatomy of the human heart and the
path that blood takes through it. Students identify the four chambers
(right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle), the four
valves (tricuspid, pulmonary, mitral/bicuspid, aortic), and the major
vessels entering and leaving the heart (superior and inferior vena cava,
pulmonary arteries and veins, aorta). The lab then traces the dual-loop
circulation that the heart drives: deoxygenated blood returning from the
body enters the right atrium, passes through the tricuspid valve into
the right ventricle, is pumped through the pulmonary valve and pulmonary
arteries to the lungs, returns oxygenated via the pulmonary veins to the
left atrium, passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle,
and is pumped through the aortic valve and aorta out to the systemic
circulation. Students see why the left ventricle has a substantially
thicker myocardium than the right — it must pump blood through the
entire body rather than just to the lungs — and how the valves prevent
backflow during the cardiac cycle.

## Learning objectives
- Students will identify and name the four chambers of the heart, the four heart valves, and the major vessels entering and leaving the heart.
- Students will trace the path of blood through the heart's dual circulation, naming each chamber, valve, and vessel in order.
- Students will distinguish the pulmonary circuit from the systemic circuit and explain why the right and left sides of the heart serve different circulatory functions.
- Students will explain why the left ventricle has a substantially thicker myocardium than the right ventricle, relating wall thickness to the pressure required to perfuse the systemic versus pulmonary circuits.
- Students will describe the function of the atrioventricular and semilunar valves in preventing backflow, and identify which valves separate which chambers or chamber-vessel pairs.

## Detailed script
The lab opens with an exterior view of the heart. A full-size human
heart appears centered in the workspace, oriented anatomically with the
apex pointing down and slightly to the left. Two copies of the heart
are displayed side by side: the left copy is intact, showing the exterior
surface and the major vessels entering and leaving (superior and inferior
vena cava, pulmonary trunk and arteries, pulmonary veins, aorta); the
right copy is a cutaway view with the anterior wall removed, exposing
the four chambers, the septum, and the four valves. Students can rotate
both heart models together to view from any angle, and narration walks
through the gross orientation: which side is the right side, which is
the left, and why the labels are reversed from the viewer's perspective
because the heart is described from the patient's own orientation.

The second scene is a guided structural tour. Working primarily with
the cutaway view, narration highlights each major structure in sequence
while a label appears beside it. The tour proceeds: the four chambers
in the order right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle;
then the four valves in the order tricuspid (between right atrium and
right ventricle), pulmonary or pulmonic (between right ventricle and
pulmonary trunk), mitral or bicuspid (between left atrium and left
ventricle), and aortic (between left ventricle and aorta); then the
major vessels in the order superior vena cava, inferior vena cava,
pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, and aorta. As each structure is
highlighted, the corresponding region of the intact heart on the left
is also highlighted, so students can map interior structures to
exterior landmarks. The narration notes the dual naming of the
mitral/bicuspid valve and explains that students will encounter both
terms in clinical and anatomical contexts.

The third scene introduces the dual-circuit color-coded blood stream.
Working with the cutaway view, the chambers and vessels fill with
flowing color: blue for deoxygenated blood (right side of heart,
pulmonary arteries, and systemic veins) and red for oxygenated blood
(left side of heart, pulmonary veins, and systemic arteries). The
stream flows continuously, showing the steady-state pattern of
circulation. Narration emphasizes the central insight: the right side
of the heart handles deoxygenated blood and pumps it to the lungs; the
left side handles oxygenated blood and pumps it to the body. The
pulmonary and systemic circuits are labeled in the scene, with the
pulmonary circuit highlighted briefly, then the systemic circuit
highlighted briefly, so students can see the two loops as distinct
units of the dual circulation.

The fourth scene is the step-by-step red blood cell trace. A single
large red blood cell appears in the superior vena cava. Students press
a "next" control to advance the cell one step along its path through
the circulation. At each step, the cell moves to the next chamber or
vessel, a label appears naming the structure it just entered, and
narration describes what is happening — "the right atrium contracts,
pushing the cell through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle";
"the right ventricle contracts, pushing the cell through the pulmonary
valve into the pulmonary trunk"; and so on. The cell traverses the
full path: right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary
valve, pulmonary trunk, pulmonary arteries, lung capillaries (where
the cell visibly changes from blue to red as it picks up oxygen),
pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic
valve, aorta, and out to systemic circulation. The cell then returns
blue through the systemic veins back to the superior vena cava,
completing the loop. Students can replay the trace as many times as
they want.

The fifth scene examines wall thickness asymmetry between the
ventricles. A side-by-side display shows two cross-sections of the
heart cut at the same horizontal level through the ventricles. On the
left, the right ventricle is highlighted, with its thin myocardium
labeled (approximately 3-5 mm thick in a healthy adult). On the
right, the left ventricle is highlighted, with its substantially
thicker myocardium labeled (approximately 10-15 mm thick). Narration
explains the functional reason for the asymmetry: the right ventricle
pumps blood only to the lungs, a low-pressure circuit, while the left
ventricle pumps blood to the entire body against much higher
resistance. The cross-sections include scale markers so students can
visually confirm the roughly three-to-one thickness ratio. Students
can rotate the cross-sections and compare directly.

The sixth scene examines valve function and the prevention of
backflow. The cutaway view returns, with the four valves highlighted.
Narration introduces the two categories: the atrioventricular valves
(tricuspid and mitral) sit between atria and ventricles and prevent
blood from flowing backward into the atria when the ventricles
contract; the semilunar valves (pulmonary and aortic) sit between
ventricles and their outflow arteries and prevent blood from flowing
backward into the ventricles when they relax. Each valve is shown in
two states — open and closed — represented by two separate models that
swap when the valve transitions. Students step through each valve's
open-and-closed states, seeing how the flap-like leaflets seal the
opening when closed and clear the path when open. The narration notes
which valves are open and which are closed at each phase of blood flow:
during ventricular contraction, the atrioventricular valves are closed
and the semilunar valves are open; during ventricular relaxation, the
reverse is true.

The seventh scene is a structure identification quiz. The cutaway
heart appears with all labels removed. The system highlights a
structure (a chamber, valve, or vessel) and presents three or four
candidate names; students select the correct name. The quiz cycles
through all four chambers, all four valves, and the major vessels
named earlier in the tour. After each selection, the correct answer
is confirmed or corrected with brief narration. Students can replay
the quiz to reinforce identification.

The eighth scene is a blood flow tracing quiz. The student is shown
a starting position (for example, "a unit of blood has just entered
the right atrium") and asked to identify the next structure in the
path of circulation from a list of options. The quiz steps through
the complete pulmonary and systemic circuit, with each correct answer
advancing to the next step and each incorrect answer prompting a
brief reminder of the path direction. The quiz tests not only the
sequence but also requires students to distinguish, for example,
between pulmonary arteries (carrying deoxygenated blood) and
pulmonary veins (carrying oxygenated blood) — a common point of
confusion since this is the opposite of the systemic circulation
pattern.

The lab closes with a recap. A summary screen lists the five concept
areas covered — the structural inventory of chambers, valves, and
vessels; the dual-circuit organization of pulmonary and systemic
circulation; the path of a unit of blood through the full circuit;
the wall thickness asymmetry between left and right ventricles; and
the function of valves in preventing backflow — with each area
highlighted in turn as the narration reviews it.
